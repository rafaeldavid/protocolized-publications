# Scholl — Move Fast and Don't Break (Safety Critical) Things, Part 1 (August 2025)

## Summary
First essay in Blake Scholl's series describing how Boom Supersonic compressed XB-1's development timeline against industry norms. XB-1 — Boom's demonstrator aircraft — was built by roughly 50 engineers at approximately $190M, an order of magnitude better than comparable aerospace programs. Scholl introduces what he calls a "Jevons's Law of engineering": when iteration is fast and cheap, more designs can be evaluated, and a much better design can be discovered. The headline example is *Boomless Cruise* — the supersonic-without-audible-boom flight regime — which was discovered through iteration, not original design objective.

## Why it matters for the CMM
Anchors the Level 5 thesis: the non-linear payoff of full protocol maturity is not "the same thing, faster" — it is the discovery of new decision spaces that are inaccessible at lower iteration speeds. Three load-bearing contributions:

1. **Jevons's Law of engineering.** When iteration cost collapses, total iteration volume rises and the range of designs evaluated expands. This is the mechanism by which Level 5 organizations discover capability that Level 1–4 organizations cannot.
2. **Boomless Cruise as the canonical "discovered, not designed" outcome.** Distinguishes maturity outcomes from input-side improvements — Level 5's payoff is novel decision space, not productivity.
3. **XB-1 unit economics.** ~50 engineers, ~$190M, order-of-magnitude advantage — concrete anchor for what infrastructural-and-above maturity looks like in a regulated, safety-critical domain.

## Claims citable from this observation
- XB-1 was built by approximately 50 engineers at roughly $190M — order of magnitude better than comparable aerospace programs
- Boomless Cruise was discovered through iteration, not as the original design objective
- "But the real magic isn't the time savings—it's sort of a Jevon's Law of engineering: when engineering iteration is quick and cheap, many more designs can be evaluated and a much better design can be discovered." (verbatim, Scholl)
