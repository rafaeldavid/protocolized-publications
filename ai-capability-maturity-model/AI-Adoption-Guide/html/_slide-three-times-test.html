Chapter 1 · More than a tool

Three times, a new technology remade the environment of work

Each time, the first adopters initially bolted the new technology onto the old architecture. But the real gains only came after a complete redesign of the production environment.

I · 1830–1870

Water → Steam

Geographic liberation of the factory

Mills ran on water wheels, which meant factories had to sit on fast-moving rivers. The steam engine severed that tether. Factories could move inland, to labor markets and raw materials rather than river banks.

This wasn't just an operational change — it terraformed cities. Industrial centers no longer needed to be riverside. New inland factory towns emerged; entire urban geographies reorganized around rail and coal, not water. The city itself was a side effect of the technology transition.

II · 1890–1920

Steam → Electricity

The floor redesign that unlocked 30 years of gains

The first wave simply swapped the steam engine for an electric motor — same central line shaft, same belts and pulleys radiating out to every machine. Output barely improved. The form of power delivery was new; the factory floor was not.

The transformation came with unit drive: one motor per machine. That required scrapping the factory floor and redesigning it around the new capability. Only then did manufacturing output per man-hour jump from 1.3%/yr to 3.1%/yr — a gain that defined the entire 20th-century economy.

III · 1960–1990

Container shipping

Tokenizing international trade into the global factory

Container shipping standardized the unit of international trade — a fixed, stackable, interoperable box. This tokenization of cargo made global supply chains predictable enough to coordinate across continents at scale.

The factory stopped being a building and became a global entity: design here, components there, final assembly elsewhere. Ports, cities, and logistics networks were rebuilt around this distributed architecture. The container remade the global geography of production.
